Winter Heating Bills 2026-27: What to Expect and Cut

By Ryan Forman · November 18, 2026

Quick answer

It depends on how you heat. In its October 2026 Winter Fuels Outlook, the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) expects homes that heat mainly with electricity to spend about 4% more this winter than last, and homes heating with natural gas to spend about 9% less. Heating oil homes could see the biggest jump, around 21%. Whatever your fuel, the fastest wins are the same: check for estimated meter reads, the wrong rate plan and fees you can get waived, then trim usage with a thermostat schedule.

Why winter bills are moving in different directions

EIA's forecast covers November through March. For electric heat, it expects residential electricity prices to rise about 3%, and with slightly higher use, bills to rise about 4%. The West could see the largest electric increase, about 9%, mainly because EIA assumes a much colder winter there than last year. On average, EIA estimates electric-heat households will spend about $1,196 this winter on electricity.

Natural gas homes get better news: lower prices push average winter spending down about 9% nationally, to roughly $640. The West is the exception, at about +7%, again because of colder weather. Propane spending is forecast down about 3%, and heating oil up about 21%.

One catch: weather drives all of this. EIA notes that NOAA has identified a strong El Niño pattern this winter, which could cause large swings from the forecast. And a lower gas price doesn't always show up on your bill right away. EIA explains that retail gas prices lag wholesale prices, and part of what you pay covers utility operating and delivery costs that aren't tied to the price of gas itself.

How to check your heating bill

  1. Find your billing period and days of service. A 34-day bill will look higher than a 28-day bill even if nothing changed.
  2. Check for "estimated" or "E" next to the meter reading. If the utility guessed your usage, the number can be high. Read your meter, take a dated photo and ask for a corrected bill.
  3. Compare usage, not just dollars. Find kWh (electric) or therms/CCF (gas) and compare them with the same month last year. Many bills show a 13-month usage chart.
  4. Find your rate plan or rate class. Make sure it matches your home (for example, residential, not commercial) and any plan you signed up for.
  5. Read the fixed charges. Look for a monthly customer or base charge and delivery charges. These are usually set by regulators and stay the same no matter how much you use.
  6. Look for one-time fees. Late fees, reconnection fees and deposit charges are easy to miss.
  7. In states with energy choice, check your contract end date. When a fixed-rate plan expires, some plans roll to a higher variable rate. (Same idea as an internet promo ending.)

What to say: a call script

Most utilities don't have a "retention department," but the same polite, specific approach works.

You: Hi, my bill this month is much higher than usual. Can you help me review it?
Rep: Sure. What's your account number?
You: [Account number]. First, was this bill based on an actual meter read or an estimate? I have a photo of my meter from [date] showing [reading].
Rep: It looks like it was estimated.
You: Then please rebill it using my actual reading, and credit any difference.
You: Next, can you check that I'm on the lowest-cost rate plan I qualify for? And do you offer budget billing or levelized billing so my winter bills are spread out?
You: Last thing: I see a late fee from [month]. I've paid on time otherwise. Could you waive it as a courtesy?
You: Thank you. Can you note these changes on my account and tell me when the corrected bill will post?

If you're in an energy-choice state and your plan has expired, ask your retail provider which fixed-rate plans you can switch to today. For phone and internet providers, use our retention department script.

Cut usage, too

The U.S. Department of Energy says you can save as much as 10% a year on heating and cooling by turning your thermostat back 7–10°F for 8 hours a day from its normal setting. A programmable or smart thermostat does this automatically while you sleep or are away.

FAQ

Will my heating bill go up this winter? If you heat with electricity or heating oil, EIA's October forecast says probably yes, by about 4% and 21% on average. Gas and propane homes are expected to spend less on average, except gas homes in the West.

Why didn't my gas bill drop when gas prices fell? Retail gas prices lag wholesale prices, and part of your bill pays for delivery and utility operating costs that don't track the price of gas.

What is budget billing? The utility averages your yearly cost into similar monthly payments, so winter bills don't spike. You still pay for what you use; it just smooths the timing.

Can I dispute an estimated bill? Yes. Read your meter, take a dated photo and ask the utility to rebill from the actual reading.

Are winter forecasts reliable? They're a broad guide. EIA says weather could cause large differences from its forecast this year.

Bottom line

This winter's bill depends on your fuel and your region, but billing errors and the wrong plan cost money in every case. Check your reads, your rate plan and your fees before the coldest months hit.

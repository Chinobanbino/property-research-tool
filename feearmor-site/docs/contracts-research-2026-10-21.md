# FeeArmor can launch with seven documents

*Research synthesis as of October 21, 2026. This is not legal advice. It is a working brief for a founder and the Texas attorney who will finalize the documents.*

> **How to read the verify flags.** The research sessions behind this report could not open many primary sources directly (statutes.capitol.texas.gov, ecfr.gov, fcc.gov and most competitor terms pages were blocked). Many findings therefore rest on search-engine excerpts of the cited page, or on well-known rule text the researchers could not reopen. Where that is the case, the claim is marked **(verify)**. Treat every (verify) item as a to-do for your attorney: confirm the current wording on the official source before relying on it. Competitor terms in particular come from search snippets, some of them old.

**Bottom line.** FeeArmor needs no Texas license that the research could find for negotiating telecom, cable and internet bills. It can launch as soon as it has seven things in place: (1) a bilingual Service Agreement signed by clickwrap, (2) a separate one-page Authorization to Act for each customer's accounts, (3) a per-change approval step, (4) an SMS consent box and texting program, (5) a bilingual privacy notice, (6) a contractor agreement and security program for the offshore VAs, and (7) a written call script and advertising file. Your biggest legal risks do not come from the contract wording. They come from what happens on the phone and in your ads. A $7/hour VA who says "I'm the account holder," reads out a customer's PIN, records a Californian without saying so, or quotes a savings example most customers never reach creates more exposure than any clause can cure. The Texas Deceptive Trade Practices Act (DTPA) cannot be waived by an ordinary online customer, so your protection comes from being accurate, not from disclaimers. The design you already have works in your favor: you bill only after savings post, charge on 12 months rather than 24, charge nothing when there are no savings, and refuse to impersonate customers. Competitors' complaint histories show that fees calculated on projected savings and unclear consent are what get services in trouble. Texas text-message registration (ch. 302) probably does not apply if you text only people who checked a consent box and do no cold outreach, but that conclusion rests on Secretary of State guidance, not on the statute itself. Six questions need a Texas attorney before launch. They are listed at the end.

## Do these eleven things before you take your first customer

The list below is ordered so you can work straight through it. Everything after this section explains why.

| # | Action today | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Write the **"verified savings" definition** and the true-up/refund rule into the Service Agreement and put the fee math next to every "No savings, no fee" headline | The most likely source of DTPA suits, chargebacks and BBB complaints (BillShark's complaints are about this) |
| 2 | Adopt an **absolute call rule**: agents say "I'm [name] from FeeArmor, calling on behalf of [customer], who has authorized us," never claim to be the customer, never handle PINs, passwords or one-time codes | Impersonation and PIN handling put your marketing claims, carrier relationships and data-breach status at risk all at once |
| 3 | Make the **3-way call with the customer authenticating live** your default method for getting through carrier security | No carrier support page found accepts a generic third-party letter of authorization for consumer accounts |
| 4 | Announce **"this call is recorded" on every call and every leg**, to everyone; stop recording if anyone objects | Operating to the all-party-consent standard covers California, Florida and the other all-party states, plus the VA's country |
| 5 | Put an **unchecked SMS consent box** with the full disclosure on the intake form; register an A2P 10DLC brand and campaigns before sending any texts | TCPA damages are $500–$1,500 per text; carriers block unregistered texts |
| 6 | **No cold calls or texts, no purchased lists.** Contact only people who reached out to you or checked the box, between **9am–9pm Mon–Sat and noon–9pm Sun**, recipient's local time | Keeps you inside the Texas ch. 302 consent-based position and the Texas calling-hours law |
| 7 | Run the **3-payment plan interest-free with no finance charge**, and list every fee you might charge (late fee, returned payment) in the agreement, or charge none | Texas Debt Collection Act bars fees the agreement does not expressly authorize; interest or more than four installments can trigger Truth in Lending |
| 8 | Publish a **bilingual privacy notice** that says contractors outside the US may access data, and that you do not sell personal data | Hiding offshore access can be a deceptive omission; selling sensitive data needs consent even for small businesses in Texas |
| 9 | Give VAs access only through a **masked, logged CRM** (no database logins, no downloads, MFA), and have each sign a confidentiality/data agreement | Reasonable-security duty under Texas law and the FTC Act; cheap Texas safe harbor if you document a framework |
| 10 | **Replace "Examples only" savings illustrations** with real typical-results figures, or hold them until you have data; archive proof for the 24-month competitor comparison | "Examples only" does not cure a misleading net impression |
| 11 | Book a **Texas attorney review** of the six open questions at the end of this report | Several decisions (arbitration, liability cap, the ch. 302 exemption) are judgment calls |

## Contracts carry the deal; the phone call carries the risk

### The customer Service Agreement: a clause-by-clause checklist

The Service Agreement is the contract that creates the fee. Because the DTPA cannot be waived and any ambiguity will be read against FeeArmor as the drafter, the clauses below lean toward clarity and fairness. That is a deliberate enforcement strategy, not just good manners. Each recommendation is drawn from the Texas enforceability, competitor-terms and collections research.

| Clause | What it should say | Recommendation and reason |
|---|---|---|
| **Parties and eligibility** | FeeArmor's legal entity name, Texas address, phone; customer is 18+ and is the account holder or legally authorized | A Texas federal court refused to enforce a clickwrap arbitration clause against a minor, because a minor's contract is voidable ([Haynes Boone, Sept. 2025](https://www.haynesboone.com/news/alerts/arbitration-in-the-fifth-september-2025)) |
| **Scope of services** | Negotiate telecom, cable, internet (and any other listed bill types) rates, fee removals, promotions, retention offers | Expressly **exclude** electricity plan selection (PUCT broker registration required for compensation under [16 TAC §25.112](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/texas/16-Tex-Admin-Code-SS-25-112)), insurance, past-due debts or settlements, and anything to do with credit. A service that offers to improve credit is a Credit Services Organization under [Tex. Fin. Code ch. 393](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/FI/htm/FI.393.htm) |
| **Fee** | 40% of the first 12 months of verified savings; one-time; $0 if no savings | Put a worked example in the clause ("If your bill drops by $20/month, your 12-month savings are $240 and the fee is $96"). Never more than 12 months, so the "12 months max" ad claim stays literally true |
| **Definition of "verified savings"** | Baseline = last pre-engagement bill for the same or equivalent services, net of taxes and one-time charges; savings = the net monthly reduction shown on a posted bill | The research flags this as **the single biggest DTPA risk** for this business model. Under the DTPA, failing to disclose known information to induce a sale is §17.46(b)(24), and misstating the benefits of a service is (b)(5) (verify) ([Tex. Bus. & Com. Code ch. 17](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/BC/htm/BC.17.htm)) |
| **Promo credits and one-time credits** | State whether one-time credits count (recommend: count once, at face value) and how promos that expire before month 12 are handled (recommend: count only the months the discount actually applies) | Rocket Money delays negotiation until an existing promo expires ([Rocket Money Help](https://help.rocketmoney.com/en/articles/9744544-when-will-the-negotiated-savings-take-effect)); BillCutterz treats one-time credits separately ([BillCutterz Terms](https://billcutterz.com/terms/)) |
| **True-up / refund** | If the provider reverses the discount, or the customer cancels or changes the underlying service mid-year, FeeArmor recalculates on savings actually realized and refunds the difference or cancels remaining installments | No competitor found offers this; BillShark's support pages only promise to "investigate" ([BillShark support](https://billshark.freshdesk.com/support/solutions/folders/6000233342)). It keeps "No savings, no fee" true in edge cases and gives you a strong chargeback and DTPA answer |
| **Billing and payment** | Invoice after savings post on a bill; pay in full or in 3 equal, interest-free installments on stated dates; card-on-file authorization with amounts, dates and retry policy | Under [Tex. Fin. Code §392.303](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._fin._code_section_392.303) you may not collect any fee not expressly authorized by the agreement. A plan with no finance charge and four or fewer installments stays outside Truth in Lending ([12 CFR 1026.2](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/chapter-X/part-1026/section-1026.2), verify) |
| **Late and returned-payment fees** | Either none, or a stated modest amount | Recommendation: **none at launch.** Each one must be in the agreement to be collectible, and an unreasonable late fee can be treated as interest (inference; counsel to confirm) |
| **Surcharges** | No card surcharges | A debit-card surcharge is barred by [Tex. Bus. & Com. Code §604A.002](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._bus._&_com._code_section_604a.002); the credit-card surcharge ban's enforceability is "unclear" after *Rowell v. Paxton* ([Texas State Law Library](https://sll.texas.gov/faqs/credit-card-surcharge/)). Offer an ACH discount instead if desired |
| **Change approval** | No plan change, contract extension, equipment purchase, added service or downgrade without the customer's express approval for that specific change (see the change-approval section below) | Rocket Money reserves the right to **upgrade** plans or add services without asking ([Rocket Money Help](https://help.rocketmoney.com/en/articles/9744533-understanding-plan-upgrades-downgrades-and-service-changes-after-bill-negotiation)); BillShark faces unverified complaints of unapproved plan changes ([ComplaintsBoard](https://www.complaintsboard.com/billshark-b128566)). Opt-in per change is a differentiator |
| **Re-negotiation** | Each new negotiation requires a new opt-in; no automatic repeat negotiations | BillShark reserves the right to repeat services automatically, with an email opt-out, and each round produces a new fee ([BillShark Terms](https://www.billshark.com/terms), snippet). Opt-out repeat fees are the clause most likely to cause "unexpected charge" complaints |
| **Cancellation** | Cancel any time; free cancellation before FeeArmor contacts any provider (recommend a 3-day no-charge window as well); after savings are secured, the fee is owed only on savings actually realized | No mandatory cooling-off period was found for a purely online sale; the FTC Cooling-Off Rule and Texas home-solicitation law reach in-person sales ([16 CFR Part 429](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-429), verify; [Tex. Bus. & Com. Code ch. 601](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/BC/pdf/BC.601.pdf)). If you ever sell at homes, fairs or pop-ups, a 3-business-day right applies, in the language of the pitch |
| **No guarantee** | FeeArmor uses reasonable efforts; savings are not guaranteed | Do not say "guaranteed savings" anywhere (DTPA §17.46(b)(5), verify) |
| **Customer representations** | Customer is the account holder or authorized; information supplied is accurate | Modeled on Pine's terms, which put authority on the user ([Pine Terms](https://www.19pine.ai/terms-of-service)) |
| **Customer indemnity** | Limited to losses from false account information or lack of authority, or omit entirely | A broad consumer indemnity invites unconscionability and DTPA attack and is rarely collectible |
| **Limitation of liability** | Cap at fees paid; exclude consequential damages; "INCLUDING CLAIMS ARISING FROM FEEARMOR'S OWN NEGLIGENCE"; carve out gross negligence, fraud, willful misconduct and non-waivable rights including the DTPA; bold all-caps heading on the signing screen | Texas requires **fair notice** (express negligence plus conspicuousness) for clauses that shift a party's own negligence. In *Dresser v. Page Petroleum* releases failed because they sat in numbered paragraphs "without headings or contrasting type" ([Casemine on Dresser](https://www.casemine.com/commentary/us/fair-notice-requirements-applied-to-exculpatory-releases-in-dresser-industries-v.-page-petroleum/view); [TAMU Real Estate Center](https://trerc.tamu.edu/article/liability-waivers-enforceable)). Keep identical formatting in the Spanish version |
| **DTPA** | **No DTPA waiver.** Optionally: "Nothing in this Agreement limits rights you have under the Texas DTPA that cannot be waived" | A consumer waiver is void unless the consumer had their own lawyer and signed a statutory-form waiver ([§17.42, Justia](https://law.justia.com/codes/texas/business-commerce-code/title-2/chapter-17/subchapter-e/section-17-42/)). A "you waive all claims" line could itself be pleaded as misrepresenting the customer's rights |
| **Dispute resolution** | Informal 30–60 day resolution step; small-claims carve-out; arbitration with class waiver *or* no arbitration (attorney decision); 30-day opt-out; FeeArmor pays fees beyond a court filing fee; mass-arbitration batching | See the dispute-resolution section below |
| **Governing law and venue** | Texas law; arbitration or hearing in the customer's county | BillCutterz, a Texas competitor, uses Texas law and Nueces County venue ([BillCutterz Terms](https://billcutterz.com/terms/)); Rocket Money arbitrates "in your county" ([Rocket Money Terms](https://www.rocketmoney.com/terms)) |
| **Changes to terms** | No unilateral changes to a signed engagement; new terms apply only to new engagements and need a new click. If you keep a change clause: 30 days' email notice, prospective only, never applied to known disputes or accrued fees, customer may reject | Texas courts treat change-at-will clauses as illusory unless limited (*In re Halliburton*; *In re 24R*). The Fifth Circuit struck clauses effective "immediately upon notice" or by website posting ([Disputing blog](https://disputingblog.com/fifth-circuit-holds-arbitration-provision-illusory-and-unenforceable); [*Carey v. 24 Hour Fitness*](https://www.ca5.uscourts.gov/Opinions/pub/10/10-20845-CV0.wpd.pdf)) |
| **Language** | "This Agreement is provided in English and Spanish. You choose the version. Both have the same meaning. If they differ, the version you selected and signed controls." | See the language section below |
| **E-sign consent** | Separate consent to electronic records meeting ESIGN §7001(c) | See the e-signature section below |
| **Communications** | Consent to transactional emails/texts about the service; marketing texts only via the separate SMS box | See the TCPA section below |
| **Recording consent** | Customer consents to recording of calls FeeArmor places or joins on the customer's behalf | Also repeated in the Authorization and verbally at the start of each call |
| **Privacy** | Link to the privacy notice; disclose that contractors outside the US may access data | See the privacy section below |
| **Notices and contact** | Email address and phone for cancellation, disputes and data requests | The Texas privacy law allows email-only request methods for online-only businesses ([Texas AG](https://www.texasattorneygeneral.gov/consumer-protection/file-consumer-complaint/consumer-privacy-rights/texas-data-privacy-and-security-act)) |

### The Authorization to Act: a limited agency letter, not a power of attorney

The Authorization to Act is a separate one- or two-page document. It gives FeeArmor actual authority as the customer's agent and serves as the customer's written request to each carrier. It should **not** be a Texas statutory durable power of attorney: those require notarization and are built for financial agency that survives incapacity ([Tex. Estates Code ch. 751](https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/resources/ES/pdf/ES.751.pdf)). That adds friction with no benefit, since carriers verify the customer live anyway. The research recommends these ten elements:

1. **Parties and identity.** The customer's full legal name exactly as it appears on each account, plus service address, phone and email. FeeArmor's legal name and Texas address. Verification at signing: the e-sign audit trail and a one-time code sent to the customer's phone.
2. **Account schedule.** Each provider, the account number (or last 4 digits), the account holder name and the services covered. New accounts are added only by a signed amendment.
3. **Enumerated scope.** Review bills and plans, negotiate rates, promotions, credits, fee waivers and retention offers. Include the sentence "This is my affirmative written request under 47 U.S.C. §222(c)(2) that [Provider] disclose my account information to FeeArmor." That statute requires a carrier to disclose customer network information "upon affirmative written request by the customer, to any person designated by the customer" ([47 U.S.C. §222](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/47/222)).
4. **Change limits.** Changes are accepted only within limits the customer sets (for example no new contract term, no equipment purchase, no downgrade), or only after real-time approval.
5. **Hard exclusions.** No SIM or eSIM changes, number ports, port-out PINs or account-lock changes. No changes to passwords, PINs, security questions or contact details of record. No adding or removing authorized users. No cancelling service or opening lines or accounts. No payments or payment-method changes. No requests for call-detail records.
6. **Truthful identification covenant.** "FeeArmor's agents will identify themselves by name as representatives of FeeArmor acting on my behalf and will never represent that they are me. FeeArmor will never ask for my account passwords; where the provider requires authentication I will provide it directly to the provider."
7. **Customer representations.** The customer is the account holder or legally authorized, with any power of attorney attached.
8. **Duration and revocation.** The authorization lasts 12 months or until the negotiation is complete, whichever comes first. It is revocable at any time by email or in-app notice. It ends on the customer's death or incapacity.
9. **Recording consent and data handling.** This includes disclosure that contractors outside the US may access account data.
10. **Copy reliance.** An electronic copy is as valid as the original. Attach a one-page provider-facing summary.

**Do not expect carriers to accept the letter on its own.** The research found official authorized-user or account-manager pages for AT&T, Verizon, T-Mobile, Xfinity and Spectrum. All five route delegation through the account holder's own app, portal or phone call. **None mentions accepting a written authorization from a third-party bill-negotiation company** for a consumer account ([AT&T KM1040456](https://www.att.com/support/article/my-account/KM1040456/); [Verizon KB 205643](https://www.verizon.com/support/knowledge-base-205643/); [T-Mobile](https://www.t-mobile.com/support/account/billing-responsible-party-and-authorized-users-consumer); [Xfinity](https://www.xfinity.com/support/articles/primary-and-secondary-accounts); [Spectrum](https://www.spectrum.net/support/account-and-billing/spectrum-account-roles-and-privileges)). Frontier and DirecTV pages were not found. BillShark's own terms say a provider "may require verbal confirmation" that BillShark is acting as the user's agent ([BillShark Terms](https://www.billshark.com/terms)). The letter's real jobs are proof of authority, consent evidence and the §222(c)(2) request. Live access comes from the **3-way call**: the provider authenticates the customer, the customer says on the record "[Agent] from FeeArmor may discuss and make changes to my account on my behalf today," and the agent proceeds openly as FeeArmor. That spoken statement is also the cleanest source of apparent authority under general agency principles. Texas case law on this point was not retrieved (verify).

Adding a FeeArmor staffer as an "authorized user" or "account manager" is technically possible at several carriers. It is a poor default. Xfinity requires the invitee to create their own login, and T-Mobile ties the user to a legal name matching photo ID. Offshore VAs would end up named on many strangers' accounts. Reserve this route, if you use it at all, for a vetted US supervisor.

### Change approval: one approval per change, in writing

Use a short per-change approval, sent by text or email with a one-tap or one-click "Approve" in the customer's language. Each approval states:

- the provider and account
- the current plan and price
- the proposed plan, price and promo end date
- any new contract term, early-termination fee or equipment cost
- any lost features or speed
- the estimated monthly saving and the resulting FeeArmor fee

Keep the approval with the call recording. This addresses the complaint pattern seen against competitors (unapproved plan changes, unexpected contract terms). No competitor clause covering consent to contract extensions was found, so this gap is yours to own.

### SMS consent: separate, unchecked, and specific

The researchers drafted SMS consent wording for counsel review. It sits under its own **unchecked** box, separate from the submit button and from acceptance of the terms:

> ☐ Yes, text me. By checking this box, I agree that FeeArmor may send me marketing and account text messages (e.g., savings offers, promo-expiration alerts, bill updates, and payment reminders) at the mobile number above, including messages sent using automated technology. Consent is not a condition of purchase. Message frequency varies. Msg & data rates may apply. Reply STOP to cancel, HELP for help. See our Terms and Privacy Policy. We do not sell or share your mobile number or SMS consent with third parties for their marketing.

Mirror this in Spanish, and send the confirmation, HELP and STOP replies in both languages. The legal basis is in the TCPA section below.

## Texas will enforce a clear clickwrap but not a DTPA waiver

**The DTPA cannot be contracted around.** A consumer's DTPA waiver is "contrary to public policy and is unenforceable and void." The only exception requires that the consumer not be in a significantly disparate bargaining position and be represented by a lawyer the business did not suggest, with a conspicuous bold waiver in statutory form ([§17.42, Justia](https://law.justia.com/codes/texas/business-commerce-code/title-2/chapter-17/subchapter-e/section-17-42/)). No clickwrap retail customer meets that test. The stakes are real. A consumer who proves a "knowing" violation can recover up to **three times economic damages plus mental-anguish damages**, and a prevailing consumer **"shall" recover attorney's fees** (§17.50, verify). Consumers must give 60 days' written notice before suing (§17.505) and have a 2-year limitations period (§17.565) (verify) ([Tex. Bus. & Com. Code ch. 17](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/BC/htm/BC.17.htm)). The Attorney General can also seek civil penalties (§17.47). Fee-shifting means a $96 fee dispute can cost thousands to defend. That is the business case for the true-up clause and a generous dispute process.

**Arbitration and class waivers are enforceable but carry a mass-arbitration cost.** Under the Federal Arbitration Act and *AT&T Mobility v. Concepcion*, a consumer class waiver in an interstate online contract is generally enforceable in Texas. The Texas Supreme Court's 2022–2023 rulings favor arbitration. In *Lennar Homes v. Rafiei*, a challenger to a delegation clause must attack that clause specifically. In *Shattenkirk*, showing that arbitration and court costs are comparable "effectively ends" a cost-based challenge ([Lennar opinion](https://www.txcourts.gov/media/1456531/220214.pdf); [LexBlog, July 2023](https://www.lexblog.com/2023/07/10/texas-supreme-court-issues-a-series-of-rulings-favoring-arbitration/)). Costs that deter claimants can still make a clause unconscionable under *In re Olshan* ([Gstexlaw](https://www.gstexlaw.com/substantive-unconscionability-and-arbitration-clauses/)). The Texas Arbitration Act excludes consumer deals of $50,000 or less unless both sides' attorneys sign, so your clause will rest on the federal Act (verify) ([Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code ch. 171](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/CP/htm/CP.171.htm)).

The practical danger is a coordinated mass filing. Under the AAA's 2024 mass-arbitration schedule, the business pays an **$8,125 initiation fee** plus per-case fees of roughly $325 and $450 for arbitrator appointment ([Mayer Brown, Feb. 2024](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2024/02/american-arbitration-association-adopts-new-mass-arbitration-rules-and-fee-schedules); [Fenwick](https://fenwick.com/insights/publications/aaa-issues-updates-to-consumer-mass-arbitration-rules-and-fee-schedule)). On the researchers' estimate, 500 claims over a disputed savings formula would cost about **$395,000 in business-side fees before any hearing**. That likely exceeds the revenue in dispute; recheck against the current schedule (verify). There are two defensible designs:

- **(a) Arbitration with protections.** Arbitration with a class waiver, a small-claims carve-out (Texas justice court hears claims up to **$20,000**, [Texas State Law Library](https://guides.sll.texas.gov/small-claims)), a mandatory individual informal-resolution step of 30–60 days, mass-arbitration batching language, FeeArmor paying fees above a court filing fee, and a **30-day email opt-out** like Rocket Money's ([Rocket Money Terms](https://www.rocketmoney.com/terms)).
- **(b) No arbitration.** No arbitration at all, relying on justice court and a Texas venue clause.

For a small business with small fees, option (b) or a strong "small claims first" version of (a) is defensible. This is an attorney decision. Whichever you pick, put a plain banner at the top of the agreement and beside the "I agree" button, mirrored in Spanish ("AVISO IMPORTANTE: ESTE CONTRATO CONTIENE UNA CLÁUSULA DE ARBITRAJE…").

**E-signatures work if you keep the evidence.** Texas law generally enforces internet agreements, and federal courts in Texas "routinely enforce" agreements where the user clicked an acknowledgment. The business bears the initial burden of proving the agreement was formed ([Haynes Boone, July 2025](https://www.haynesboone.com/news/alerts/arbitration-in-the-fifth-july-2025); [Haynes Boone, Aug. 2024](https://www.haynesboone.com/news/alerts/arbitration-in-the-fifth---august-2024)). Use a true clickwrap: an unchecked box reading "I have read and agree to the Service Agreement [link], including the arbitration and class-action waiver," with the full text viewable in the chosen language before signing. Avoid "by using this site you agree" browsewrap. Federal ESIGN and Texas UETA give e-signatures legal effect. Under UETA, attribution can be proved by "the efficacy of a security procedure," which a one-time phone code supplies (§322.009, verify) ([Tex. Bus. & Com. Code ch. 322](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/BC/htm/BC.322.htm)). The formal ESIGN §7001(c) consumer-consent disclosure is strictly required only where another law demands a writing to the consumer ([15 U.S.C. §7001](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/7001), verify). It is cheap insurance anyway. It covers the right to paper copies, how to withdraw consent, the scope of consent, how to update contact details, and hardware/software requirements.

**Keep this evidence for every customer:**

- timestamp, IP address and device/user-agent
- one-time-code verification of phone or email
- the exact terms version ID, hash and screenshots of the flow
- the language displayed and selected
- the checkbox click log
- the ESIGN consent record
- the e-sign vendor's audit trail
- delivery receipt for the emailed PDF copy in the signing language

**For Spanish-speaking customers, the version they signed should control.** The research found **no general Texas statute like California Civil Code §1632** requiring a translation whenever a consumer contract is negotiated in Spanish ([Cal. SB 633 analysis](https://sjud.senate.ca.gov/sites/sjud.senate.ca.gov/files/sb_633_limon_sjud_analysis.pdf)). Texas has targeted rules only:

- executory contracts for residential property ([Prop. Code §5.068 analysis](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/77R/analysis/html/SB00198E.htm))
- home-solicitation sales, where the contract and cancellation notice must be in the language of the oral pitch ([ch. 601](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/BC/pdf/BC.601.pdf))
- certain consumer-loan disclosures ([7 TAC §90.703](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/texas/7-Tex-Admin-Code-SS-90-703))

None applies directly to an online bill-negotiation sale. Even so, a flat "English controls" clause for a customer you marketed to and signed in Spanish invites three problems. It undercuts assent to the arbitration clause, it supports a DTPA non-disclosure claim, and it gets construed against you as drafter. Have a professional legal translator prepare the Spanish version and keep their certification. Make the controlling version the one the customer selected and signed. Texas case law on bilingual contracts was not confirmed (a commonly cited El Paso case holding that inability to read English does not excuse a signer is from memory, verify).

## Telecom rules make the honest 3-way call the only safe default

**CPNI rules bind carriers, not FeeArmor, but they decide how every call goes.** CPNI is the customer network information that carriers must protect. Carriers must "properly authenticate a customer" before discussing it by phone. Call-detail records (numbers, times, durations) need a password that is not based on biographical information ([eCFR 47 CFR §64.2010](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-64/subpart-U/section-64.2010)). Carriers must also notify customers when passwords, backup questions or the address of record change, and the notice cannot go to the new contact information (§64.2010(f), verify). Negotiating a rate never needs call detail, so ban agents from asking for it.

The FCC's 2023 **SIM-swap/port-out order** requires secure authentication before SIM changes and number ports ([FCC Fact Sheet](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DOC-397990A1.pdf); [Federal Register 2023-26338](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2023/12/08/2023-26338/protecting-consumers-from-sim-swap-and-port-out-fraud)). Compliance was waived in July 2024 pending OMB approval and a published compliance date ([FCC DA 24-649](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-24-649A1.pdf)). The current compliance date was **not confirmed** (verify in WC Docket 21-341). Either way, an agent asking about SIMs, eSIMs, ports or port-out PINs looks exactly like a fraudster, so those requests are on the hard-exclusion list.

**Never let an agent claim to be the customer.** The FTC's Impersonation Rule (16 CFR Part 461, effective April 2024) covers impersonating **governments and businesses**. The 2024 proposal to extend it to impersonating **individuals** has not been finalized on the best available evidence. The FTC's newest step, an ANPRM published Oct. 1, 2026, concerns online platforms and creates no new obligations ([Federal Register 2026-20143](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/10/01/2026-20143/rule-on-impersonation-of-government-and-businesses); [Mayer Brown, Sept. 2026](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/09/ftc-seeks-public-comment-on-whether-to-update-rule-on-impersonation-of-government-and-businesses-to-address-platforms-role-in-promoting-impersonation-scams)). The docket status of the individual provision was not confirmed (verify, FTC docket R207000).

That gap does not make posing as the customer safe. The carrier is the party being misled, and customer consent does not cure that. The exposure includes FTC Act §5 deception, and potentially wire fraud ([18 U.S.C. §1343](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1343)), Texas identifying-information offenses ([Tex. Penal Code ch. 32](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/PE/htm/PE.32.htm)) and federal telephone-records pretexting law (18 U.S.C. §1039) (all verify). The researchers found no case prosecuting a consensual bill-negotiation service. The more realistic risks are flagged or closed customer accounts, lost promotions, and your own "we never pretend to be you" ad becoming a deceptive claim the first time a VA cuts the corner. Part 461 does apply today if an agent ever implies they are calling *from* a carrier or a government agency.

Competitors split on this point. BillSmart's terms say its agents may present themselves as the account holder ([BillSmart TOS](https://getbillsmart.com/tos/)). The coordinator saw similar language in BillShark's live terms on Oct. 21, 2026, but search snippets of BillShark's current terms instead show the "verbal confirmation" agent language (**unverified**). Do not copy either.

**Caller ID and telephony.** Place calls from US numbers through your own telephony provider with proper STIR/SHAKEN attestation. Never spoof the customer's number. The Truth in Caller ID Act bars spoofing with intent to defraud or wrongfully obtain anything of value ([47 U.S.C. §227(e)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/47/227), verify). Pine uses the user's own number as caller ID with consent ([Pine Terms](https://www.19pine.ai/terms-of-service)). FeeArmor should not, because its VAs are not the customer.

## Recording, texting and registration: Texas is stricter than federal law

**Record to the all-party standard, not the Texas one.** Texas allows recording with one party's consent ([Tex. Penal Code §16.02](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/PE/htm/PE.16.htm); [RCFP Texas](https://www.rcfp.org/reporters-recording-guide/texas/)), and federal law is similar ([18 U.S.C. §2511](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/2511), verify). Illegal interception in Texas is generally a felony. The civil remedy under Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code ch. 123 is described as **$10,000 statutory damages per occurrence** plus punitive damages and fees (section number and amount, verify) ([ch. 123](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/CP/htm/CP.123.htm)).

Your calls will cross borders. In *Kearney v. Salomon Smith Barney*, California's all-party rule applied to an out-of-state firm's undisclosed recording of Californians. The court also held that advising all parties at the outset avoids liability ([Justia](https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/2006/s124739.html)). All-party states commonly listed include California, Florida, Illinois, Maryland, Massachusetts, Montana, New Hampshire, Pennsylvania and Washington, with Delaware, Connecticut, Nevada and Michigan disputed or partial. These lists come from secondary sources (verify) ([RecordingLaw.com](https://www.recordinglaw.com/united-states-recording-laws/)). The VA's own country may also require all-party consent; the Philippines is commonly described that way (not verified).

The rule, therefore: get consent in the Authorization, ask the customer verbally at the start, and announce recording to the carrier rep on every leg. Use this opening, in English or Spanish:

> "Hi, my name is [first name], I'm a representative of FeeArmor calling on behalf of your customer [name], who has authorized us. This call is being recorded for quality and verification purposes."
>
> "Hola, me llamo [nombre], soy representante de FeeArmor y llamo en nombre de su cliente [nombre], quien nos ha autorizado. Esta llamada está siendo grabada para fines de calidad y verificación."

If a rep objects, stop recording that leg or reschedule. Never record secretly. Pause or redact recordings while the customer speaks PINs, restrict access, and keep recordings 12–24 months.

**TCPA: written consent for marketing texts, any reasonable opt-out honored within 10 business days.** Marketing texts and autodialed or prerecorded marketing calls to cell phones require **prior express written consent**. That consent must name the number, authorize automated marketing, and state that consent is not a condition of purchase ([47 CFR §64.1200](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-64/subpart-L/section-64.1200), verify). Damages are **$500 per violation, up to $1,500 if willful** ([47 U.S.C. §227](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/47/227), verify). Texas ch. 305 gives a parallel state-court action for TCPA violations ([ch. 305](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/BC/htm/BC.305.htm), verify amounts).

Three 2025–2026 changes matter:

- **The one-to-one consent rule never took effect.** The 11th Circuit vacated it on Jan. 24, 2025 ([Reed Smith](https://www.reedsmith.com/our-insights/blogs/technology-law-dispatch/102k2uj/eleventh-circuit-vacates-fcc-one-to-one-consent-rule/); [MoFo](https://mofo.com/resources/insights/250130-eleventh-circuit-vacates-fcc-s-tcpa-one-to-one-consent-rule)). It does not matter to you as a single seller, but never buy or share leads.
- **Revocation by "any reasonable means" has applied since April 2025**, with a 10-business-day deadline ([Kelley Drye](https://www.kelleydrye.com/viewpoints/blogs/ad-law-access/important-robocall-regulatory-updates-fcc-extends-waiver-of-tcpa-revoke-all-rule-and-publishes-changes-to-robocall-mitigation-database-rules)).
- **FCC 26-67, adopted Sept. 30, 2026, is not yet in effect.** It will let you designate exclusive opt-out methods, and a marketing opt-out will kill all marketing robocalls and robotexts. It takes effect 30 days after Federal Register publication, which had not been found as of mid-October 2026 ([FCC 26-67](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/FCC-26-67A1.pdf); [Nixon Peabody](https://www.nixonpeabody.com/insights/alerts/2026/10/07/fcc-scraps-revoke-all-and-lets-businesses-pick-how-consumers-opt-out)). Launch under the current rules and watch for publication.

What this means in practice:

- A lead who did not check the box gets **no marketing texts**. They can receive a manually dialed live follow-up call: their inquiry creates a 3-month established business relationship that also overcomes national Do-Not-Call registration (verify).
- Texts about a customer's own negotiation are informational.
- A "your promo is expiring, let us renegotiate" text is dual-purpose, so treat it as marketing.
- Keep consent records **at least 5 years** (verify). The TCPA limitations period is 4 years, and the Telemarketing Sales Rule's record-keeping rule is 5 years ([16 CFR 310.5](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-310/section-310.5)).
- Maintain a written internal Do-Not-Call policy.

**Carrier registration for texting.** Register a brand and two campaigns (account notifications and marketing) with The Campaign Registry through your SMS provider. Indicative fees are about $4 one-time per brand and $10/month per standard campaign at Bandwidth, or a $46 one-time brand fee at Twilio ([Bandwidth](https://www.bandwidth.com/blog/how-to-register-with-10dlc-to-avoid-unnecessary-costs-in-2022/); [Twilio](https://support.twilio.com/hc/en-us/articles/1260803965530)). Host the opt-in screenshot on a public URL, and state in the privacy policy that SMS opt-in data is never shared for marketing (CTIA practice, verify) ([CTIA](https://www.ctia.org/the-wireless-industry/industry-commitments/messaging-interoperability-sms-mms)).

**Texas hours.** Texas bars consumer solicitation calls before **9am or after 9pm Monday–Saturday, and before noon or after 9pm Sunday**. It exempts calls at the consumer's request and calls about an existing contract or debt ([Tex. Bus. & Com. Code §301.051](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/SOTWDocs/BC/htm/BC.301.htm); [Texas State Law Library](https://sll.texas.gov/faqs/how-late-can-telemarketers-call)). That window is narrower than the federal 8am–9pm. Use it for all marketing calls and texts. Whether it covers texts is unresolved.

**Texas telephone solicitation registration (ch. 302 and SB 140): probably not required, if you stay consent-only.** SB 140 took effect Sept. 1, 2025. It extended ch. 302's "telephone solicitation" to text messages and made ch. 302 violations actionable under the DTPA ([Kelley Drye](https://www.kelleydrye.com/viewpoints/blogs/ad-law-access/private-right-of-action-exposure-increase-texas-expands-damages-for-telemarketing-violations-effective-september-1-2025); [Nelson Mullins](https://www.nelsonmullins.com/insights/alerts/fcc-download/all/texts-are-telemarketing-in-texas-sb-140-set-to-place-new-requirements-on-marketing-communications)). After the November 2025 settlement of the industry lawsuit, the Secretary of State's position is that "any business that sends text messages with prior consent of the consumer is not required to complete the Telephone Solicitation Registration Statement" ([Texas SOS FAQ](https://www.sos.texas.gov/statdoc/faqs3400.shtml); [Sidley, Dec. 2025](https://datamatters.sidley.com/2025/12/03/texting-in-texas-texas-ag-settlement-clarifies-no-registration-needed-for-consent-based-text-messaging/)). That guidance does not bind courts. The SOS says the decision to register is the seller's. An Attorney General opinion was reported pending and has not been confirmed.

The statutory exemption for current or former customers reportedly requires operating **under the same name for 2 years**, which a new business cannot meet (verify) ([ch. 302](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/docs/BC/htm/BC.302.htm)). Conclusion: if every marketing text goes to a box-checker, every call is a follow-up to a consumer-initiated inquiry, and existing-customer messages concern their account, document that rationale in a one-page memo and do not register. If you plan **any** cold outreach or list purchases, register first: **$200 per year plus $10,000 security** (bond, letter of credit or CD), annual renewal and periodic reports ([§302.107](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._bus._&_com._code_section_302.107)). Recheck the SOS page on launch day.

**Email.** Marketing emails need an accurate sender, a non-deceptive subject, ad identification, a **physical postal address** and an unsubscribe honored within 10 business days. Penalties reach **$53,088 per email** ([FTC CAN-SPAM guide](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business)). Invoices and savings notices are transactional, so keep their primary purpose transactional.

## Collecting your own fee puts you under the Texas Debt Collection Act

The federal FDCPA generally does not reach a creditor collecting its own debt in its own name ([15 U.S.C. §1692a](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/1692a)). **The Texas Debt Collection Act does.** Its "debt collector" definition covers anyone who directly or indirectly engages in debt collection, including original creditors ([Tex. Fin. Code §392.001](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._fin._code_section_392.001)). FeeArmor does **not** need the surety bond, which applies only to third-party collectors and credit bureaus ([ch. 392](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/docs/FI/htm/FI.392.htm)). It must avoid the following (§§392.301–.304 detail, verify):

- collecting any fee not expressly authorized by the agreement (§392.303)
- threats, except a genuine threat to sue or exercise a contract right (§392.301(b))
- harassment: abusive language, repeated calls meant to harass, calls without identifying the caller (§392.302)
- deception: using a name other than its true name, or misstating the amount owed (§392.304)

A successful plaintiff recovers actual damages and attorney's fees, with a $100-per-violation minimum for some sections ([§392.403](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._fin._code_section_392.403)). A TDCA violation is reportedly also actionable under the DTPA (§392.404, verify).

Your planned cadence is low-frequency and fine: day 0, 3, 7 and 14 reminders, then a personal call at day 45. Collect as "FeeArmor," have callers give their names, honor channel and time requests, and only threaten suit when you actually intend to file. If you do sue, a Texas **justice court small-claims case** handles claims up to $20,000. Filing fees run around **$54 plus service** in at least one county ([Williamson County](https://www.wilcotx.gov/DocumentCenter/View/15900)). An LLC may generally appear through an owner or employee under TRCP 500.4(b) (verify). Bring the signed agreement, before-and-after bills, the invoice, the payment history and the demand letter.

For chargebacks (Visa 13.1/13.3, Mastercard 4853/4837), use a recognizable billing descriptor such as "FEEARMOR SAVINGS FEE" and send a pre-charge notice before each installment. Keep a per-charge evidence pack: agreement and card-on-file authorization, provider confirmation of the new rate, the savings calculation shown to the customer, and the reminder log ([Chargeflow](https://docs.chargeflow.io/docs/reference/concepts/evidence-examples); [Kount](https://kount.com/fr/node/1098)). Confirm network rules with your acquirer (verify).

## Privacy law is light today; offshore data security is where you are exposed

**Texas privacy law mostly exempts you.** The Texas Data Privacy and Security Act exempts SBA-defined small businesses, which a launch-stage FeeArmor almost certainly is. One exception applies to everyone: a small business may not **sell sensitive personal data without the consumer's prior consent** ([Texas AG TDPSA page](https://www.texasattorneygeneral.gov/consumer-protection/file-consumer-complaint/consumer-privacy-rights/texas-data-privacy-and-security-act); [§541.107](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._bus._&_com._code_section_541.107)). Bills and contact details are mostly not "sensitive data" (verify §541.001). Do not collect immigration status. Treat preferred language carefully, because it can act as a proxy for national origin. Do not run lead-sharing or ad pixels that could count as a "sale" without review.

The AG enforces actively. It filed the first lawsuit under any state comprehensive privacy law, against Allstate/Arity, in January 2025, seeking up to $7,500 per violation ([WilmerHale](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20250121-texas-ag-brings-first-ever-lawsuit-under-a-state-comprehensive-privacy-law)). Publish a privacy notice meeting the Act's content list anyway, in English and Spanish ([§541.102](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._bus._&_com._code_section_541.102)). It should cover:

- the categories of data and the purposes for using it
- the categories of recipients, including **contractors outside the US**
- "we do not sell personal data or use it for targeted advertising"
- retention periods
- access, correction and deletion rights with a 45-day response time and a 60-day appeal
- a not-for-under-18s statement
- contact details and an effective date

Out-of-state privacy laws mostly trigger at 100,000 consumers or, for California, about **$26.6 million in revenue** ([CPPA](https://www.cppa.ca.gov/regulations/cpi_adjustment.html)). Every state's breach-notification law applies regardless of size.

**Breach law: keep PINs out, and plan for 60 and 30 days.** Under Texas ch. 521, "sensitive personal information" includes a name plus an account number **"in combination with any required security code, access code, or password"** ([§521.002](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._bus._&_com._code_section_521.002)). Names and telecom account numbers alone probably do not trigger notice. Collecting carrier PINs, passcodes or SSN last-4 would likely change that. Whether a telecom PIN counts as access to a "financial account" is unresolved, so treat it conservatively.

The deadlines are 60 days to notify individuals and **30 days to the AG if 250 or more Texans are affected** ([SB 768](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/88R/billtext/html/SB00768F.HTM); [§521.053](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._bus._&_com._code_section_521.053)). Penalties of $2,000–$50,000 per violation are also reported (verify). Every business must keep "reasonable procedures" (§521.052, verify). The new **ch. 542 (SB 2610, 2025)** bars exemplary damages against businesses with fewer than 250 employees that keep a cybersecurity program conforming to a recognized framework such as the CIS Controls ([SB 2610](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/89R/billtext/html/SB02610F.HTM); [CIS](https://www.cisecurity.org/about-us/media/press-release/texas-becomes-fifth-safe-harbor-state)). Documenting CIS Implementation Group 1 is the cheapest legal protection on this list.

**The FTC Safeguards Rule probably does not apply, but build to it anyway.** Bill negotiation is not lending, payments, collections or investment advice. The rule is activity-based, though, and lists "credit counselors and other financial advisors" and "finders" as examples ([FTC guidance](https://consumerfinance.sc.gov/sites/consumerfinance/files/Documents/FTC%20Safeguards%20Rule_%20What%20Your%20Business%20Needs%20to%20Know%20_%20Federal%20Trade%20Commission.pdf); [Troutman](https://www.troutman.com/insights/ftc-amends-safeguards-rule-for-covered-financial-institutions/)). Budgeting advice, referral fees or fee financing would raise the risk. The FTC can police unreasonable security as unfair under §5 regardless. A written information security program aligned with the Safeguards Rule and mapped to CIS IG1 serves all three regimes with one document.

**Offshore VAs: no law found bars them, so contracts and tooling carry the weight.** FCC CPNI rules and national-security mitigation agreements bind carriers, not a customer's agent. The FCC's January 2026 Marlink settlement ($175,000) shows how seriously regulators take foreign access to US customer data in carrier contexts ([Mayer Brown, Jan. 2026](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/01/fcc-enforces-team-telecom-mitigation-commitments-against-marlink)). Required controls:

- VAs work only through a role-based CRM, never direct database credentials
- account numbers are masked to the last 4 digits, and every reveal is logged
- bill uploads are auto-redacted (PINs, SSNs, card numbers), and only summary pages are requested
- a managed browser or VDI with download, print and clipboard limits
- single sign-on with MFA, no shared logins, same-day offboarding
- a company softphone, and no WhatsApp or personal email for customer data
- alerts on bulk record viewing
- deletion of bill images 12–18 months after the engagement closes

Each VA, or the VA agency, signs a contractor agreement. Model it on Texas processor-contract terms (verify §541.104):

- use of data only on FeeArmor's instructions
- confidentiality
- no subcontracting without consent
- incident notice within 24–72 hours
- return or deletion with certification
- audit and cooperation rights
- the impersonation and PIN bans
- training acknowledgment
- Texas law and venue

Disclose offshore access in the privacy notice and the Authorization. Hiding it while marketing otherwise is a deception risk under §5 and the DTPA. A US supervisor should spot-audit recordings weekly for the impersonation ban, the recording announcement and the exclusions list.

## Every ad claim needs proof on file before it runs

Under the FTC's substantiation doctrine, an objective claim made without a reasonable basis in hand is deceptive ([FTC Policy Statement](https://www.ftc.gov/node/52431)). Texas's DTPA separately lists "false or misleading statements of fact concerning the reasons for, existence of, or amount of price reductions" ([§17.46, Justia](https://law.justia.com/codes/texas/business-commerce-code/title-2/chapter-17/subchapter-e/section-17-46/)). Here is how that applies to your planned claims:

| Claim | Risk | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| "$0 upfront. No savings, no fee." | Becomes false if a fee survives a clawed-back credit, early-expiring discount or cancelled service | Adopt the true-up clause; put "40% of the first 12 months of verified savings" next to the headline; under the FTC "Free" Guide, all conditions must appear at the outset ([16 CFR Part 251](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/rules/guide-concerning-use-word-free-similar-representations)) |
| "12 months max. Some charge on up to 24." | "Max" must hold in every contract; the competitor half needs evidence | BillShark's terms define a Savings Period "up to a maximum of 24 months" ([BillShark Terms](https://www.billshark.com/terms); [BillShark FAQ](https://www.billshark.com/support)). A July 2026 news report said 12 months ([Atlanta News First](https://www.atlantanewsfirst.com/2026/07/10/online-bill-negotiators-promise-savings-require-personal-data-access/)) and the indexed terms may be old, so **screenshot and archive the live page with a date** and re-verify periodically |
| "A 40% service vs. a 50% service" | In a small market, an unnamed rival is still identifiable, so FTC comparative-ad policy and Lanham Act competitor suits apply ([16 CFR §14.15](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/14.15)) | Compare like with like (12 vs. 24 months, subscription fees); date-stamp it; don't imply all rivals charge 40–50% |
| Savings examples labeled "Examples only" | Under the 2023 Endorsement Guides a depicted result is read as typical, and a disclaimer does not cure that (16 CFR §255.2, verify) | Use real median savings with methodology on file, or a clear "typical customers save about $X"; never present invented customers as real |
| "We never ask for your passwords" / "We never pretend to be you" | Absolute operational claims, made false by one VA lapse | Enforce through intake, scripts, contracts, audits; if you ever need PINs, narrow the wording |
| Reviews and testimonials | The FTC Reviews Rule bans fake or AI-written reviews, sentiment-conditioned incentives and suppression; the first warning letters went out in Dec. 2025 at up to $53,088 per violation ([FTC warning template](https://search.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/2025-Fake-Review-Warning-Template.pdf); [Crowell](https://crowell.com/en/insights/client-alerts/final-rule-announced-the-ftc-strengthens-its-enforcement-capacity-against-deceptive-reviews-and-testimonials)) | Real customers only, disclose incentives, keep proof |
| Spanish ads and site | A required disclosure must appear in the ad's language ([16 CFR §14.9](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-A/part-14/section-14.9)) | Mirror every fee term, condition and typical-results statement in Spanish |

The FTC's junk-fees rule (Part 464) covers only live-event tickets and short-term lodging, so it does not apply to you ([FTC FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/rule-unfair-or-deceptive-fees-frequently-asked-questions)). The FTC's click-to-cancel rule was vacated by the Eighth Circuit in July 2025 ([Kirkland](https://www.kirkland.com/publications/kirkland-alert/2025/07/eighth-circuit-blocks-ftcs-click-to-cancel-rule)). Section 5, the DTPA and ROSCA still police hidden fees and negative-option billing.

The nine operating rules:

1. Agents identify as FeeArmor and never as the customer.
2. Agents never request, hear or store passwords, PINs or one-time codes. The customer speaks them directly to the carrier.
3. The default method is the 3-way call with live customer authentication.
4. Recording is announced to everyone on every leg.
5. No SIM, port, security-setting, authorized-user, cancellation or payment changes.
6. Every plan change needs a written approval for that change.
7. Marketing texts go only to box-checkers. All marketing contact happens 9am–9pm Mon–Sat and noon–9pm Sun, and opt-outs are honored by any reasonable means within 10 business days.
8. No cold outreach without first registering under ch. 302.
9. Fees are billed only after savings post, with true-up on reversal, and collection uses the true name and no empty threats.

## Six questions only a Texas attorney should answer

1. **Arbitration or no arbitration.** Choose between arbitration with a class waiver, opt-out and mass-arbitration protections, and a justice-court-first design with no arbitration. Confirm the current AAA (or JAMS/NAM) consumer fee schedules and the enforceability of a clickwrap liability cap against a negligence claim (no Texas Supreme Court clickwrap decision was found).
2. **Chapter 302 exemption memo.** Confirm the consent-only texting position against current SOS guidance and any AG opinion. Confirm the wording of the §302.058 customer exemption. Decide whether registration ($200 plus $10,000 security) is cheap enough to buy certainty.
3. **The fee and payment terms.** Review the "verified savings" definition, the true-up clause, the 3-payment plan (no finance charge, under TILA's four-installment line), any late fee under usury rules, and surcharge policy after *Rowell*. Check whether Texas sales tax applies to the fee, which no research covered.
4. **Bilingual controlling-language clause and translation.** Confirm Texas case law on bilingual consumer contracts, and that no 2025–2026 statute requires Spanish versions for this service.
5. **Regulatory perimeter.** Confirm that no Texas license applies. Check debt-management rules (Fin. Code ch. 394) if you ever negotiate past-due balances, the GLBA Safeguards Rule if you add budgeting, referral or financing features, and PUCT and TDI rules if you add electricity or insurance.
6. **Verify every (verify) citation.** In particular: DTPA §§17.46/17.50 numbering and any 2025 amendments, ch. 521 penalty figures, the ch. 123 recording damages section, TDCA §§392.301–.304 text, TRCP 500.4(b), the FCC 26-67 effective date and the SIM-swap compliance date.

## Conclusion

FeeArmor's legal position is better than its paperwork problem suggests. The two features most likely to draw Texas suits against a bill negotiator are fees on projected savings and murky consent, and your model avoids both by design. The work left is mostly operational. A bilingual clickwrap with a precise savings definition, a narrow Authorization to Act, and an honest 3-way-call script will do more than any arbitration clause, because the DTPA makes accuracy the only defense that cannot be waived.

The less obvious insight concerns your offshore VAs. They are a cost advantage, but they are also the single point where three of your marketing promises ("no passwords," "never pretend to be you," "your data is safe") can fail at once. Treat them as a controlled channel: no secrets reach them, every call is recorded and announced, and a US supervisor samples the tape. If you do, the compliance program and the brand promise become the same thing, and that is hard for competitors relying on "act as the account holder" clauses to copy.
